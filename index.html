Good science takes more than finding the right paper. It takes the judgment to know what the evidence means, and what to do when it doesn't add up.

We're building Stemma to investigate whether AI can become a more thoughtful partner in biological research. Someone to question an assumption, follow the data, weigh another explanation, and help work out what experiment should come next.

01 /The question behind the work

What happens after the search is over?

Most of the difficult thinking in science begins once you've found the information.

Two papers may disagree for good reasons. A result can be statistically striking and biologically unconvincing. The experiment you want to run may not distinguish the explanations you care about.

We want Stemma to take those moments seriously. Not to produce more confident prose, but to help researchers examine what was actually measured, discover what may have been missed, and make a more informed next move.

02 /Where we are putting the effort

Less certainty.
More judgment.

We are not claiming to have invented paper search, data analysis or AI-generated hypotheses. The field already has capable tools for all three. We are interested in what happens when scientific decisions get harder.

What makes this different ↗
01

Read the experiment, not just the abstract.

Where did the observation come from? What did the controls establish? Does the claim travel further than the evidence allows?

02

Keep more than one explanation alive.

We want a system that considers real alternatives, including less obvious ones, and knows when the data cannot separate them.

03

Ask what would change the answer.

The next experiment should tell us something important. Ideally, it should make competing explanations disagree.

04

Change your mind when the evidence does.

Good research leaves a trail of reasoning, corrections and unresolved questions. It should be possible to see what changed and why.

04 /Why this matters

For discoveries we can't yet imagine.

Scientific preparedness through discovery

The question that matters twenty years from now might look unimportant today.

That's one reason we're interested in all of biology, including the fundamental work whose value we cannot predict in advance. The more deeply we understand living systems, the better prepared we may be for questions and challenges we haven't met yet.

That is the direction. Today's Stemma is much smaller: an experimental research system whose scientific value still needs to be demonstrated.
