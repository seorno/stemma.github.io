Unknown is not the same as negative.

A claim can fail because evidence contradicts it. It can also remain unresolved because the relevant evidence was never measured well enough.

Stemma · October 2026 · 4 min read

Scientific decisions become distorted when “we do not know” is silently translated into “no”. This is especially easy in high-dimensional data, where missing coverage, low detection or mismatched contexts can create the appearance of absence.

Negative evidence makes a claim weaker.

Negative evidence requires that the experiment had a meaningful opportunity to observe the expected signal and instead observed something inconsistent with it.

Missing evidence means the question is still open.

If the relevant context was not sampled, the feature could not be measured reliably, or the comparison was not appropriate, the result should not be treated as a biological refutation.

Unknown is not a weaker version of “no”. It is a different state of knowledge.

Keeping these categories separate makes the next experiment easier to design because it tells the team whether it needs replication, a different context, deeper measurement or a genuinely competing biological hypothesis.
