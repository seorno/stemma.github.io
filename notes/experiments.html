Which experiment is worth doing next?

When two explanations fit the facts, the best next move is not always more of the same data.

From the Ontosma notebook
Vol. 01 / No. 03
← All research notes

Research gets particularly interesting when two different explanations both seem plausible. It is tempting to collect more data and hope the answer becomes obvious. Sometimes it does. Often it does not.

Suppose a treatment changes a cellular phenotype. One account says the effect is driven by a signaling pathway. Another says it reflects a broad stress response. Both might predict that the measured marker changes after treatment.

Look for the point of disagreement.

If the two explanations predict the same thing in the original assay, repeating that assay more precisely may not resolve the argument. A more useful experiment asks what each explanation predicts under a new intervention, time point, context or control.

Perhaps one explanation predicts an early, pathway-specific effect while the other predicts a delayed, general response. Perhaps removing a proposed mediator should abolish the effect under one mechanism but leave it largely intact under the other.

The most valuable experiment may be the one in which the hypotheses expect different results.

A good experimental proposal has to earn its complexity.

Distinct predictions are only part of the story. The proposed measurement needs an interpretable assay, suitable controls, enough independent replication and a realistic account of confounding. An elaborate study that cannot separate its explanations is not automatically better than a smaller, well-aimed one.

The wider scientific context matters, too. Ideas from mathematics, engineering, physics or chemistry can sometimes suggest a useful new measurement. But the connection must translate into something biological that could be observed or falsified.

Where we want to go

One of our central questions is whether Ontosma can help researchers recognize these points of disagreement and propose tests that really would change the conclusion. Creating many hypotheses is easy to imitate. Choosing between them responsibly is harder.

For now, that remains the target of the research. We intend to evaluate the choices against strong AI systems and independent scientific scrutiny, not simply count how many ideas a model can produce.
