The quiet problem with biological replicates.

A large number of measurements is not the same as a large number of independent experiments.

From the Stemma notebook
Vol. 01 / No. 02
← All research notes

A dataset can contain an extraordinary number of observations and still tell us less than it first appears to. The number of rows is not always the number that matters.

Take a simple example. Imagine cells measured from four donors. If each donor contributes thousands of cells, we may have an excellent view of those particular samples. But we still have only four independent donors for questions about differences between people.

Which things were independently assigned?

The right unit depends on the scientific question and experimental design. In some studies, it is a person or an animal. In others, it may be an independently treated culture, a field plot, a microcosm or a repeated experiment. Measurements taken within one unit often share conditions that make them correlated.

Treating those repeated measurements as independent evidence can shrink uncertainty for the wrong reason. The statistics may become impressive without the study becoming more informative about the population we want to understand.

A thousand cells are not automatically a thousand independent experiments.

Precision and generality are different things.

Having many cells can help us understand variation inside a sample. Having more independent samples helps us assess whether an effect holds beyond that sample. Both matter, but they answer different questions.

A careful analysis can model the nested structure, block by donor where appropriate, and explain the uncertainty that remains. No analytical trick can manufacture independent replication that the experiment never had.

What Stemma should learn to notice

We want the system to ask where the replicates came from, what received the intervention, which measurements are repeated, and how far the conclusion is being extended. This is one of the ordinary, essential forms of scientific judgment that an assistant should not skip.

It is also a reminder that good science is not a contest to produce the smallest p-value. The important thing is understanding what the study genuinely allows us to say.
