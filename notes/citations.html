A citation is not the same as evidence.

How a genuine paper can still be used to justify a claim it never tested.

From the Stemma notebook
Vol. 01 / No. 01
← All research notes

You can cite a real paper and still be wrong about what it proves. That sounds obvious. In practice, it is surprisingly easy to forget once a reference sits neatly beside a confident sentence.

A scientific paper answers questions about the particular system it studied. It has an organism, a model, a time frame, an intervention, a comparison group and some measured outcomes. Move beyond any one of those and the argument can change.

Finding the source is only the beginning.

Imagine a study that measures gene expression after a treatment in one cultured cell line. It might be valuable evidence about the response of that line under those conditions. It does not, by itself, establish that the treatment produces the same response in a patient, or that a change in RNA led to a functional outcome.

The problem is not necessarily bad science. The original authors may have described the limits carefully. The overreach can happen later, when a summary travels further than the experiment.

The question is not only “Can I cite this?” It is “What, exactly, did they measure?”

What should a scientific assistant actually check?

Before using a result, it should understand the experimental unit, the controls, the endpoint, the conditions and the kind of inference being made. Association does not automatically become causation. A proposed mechanism is not the same as a demonstrated one. And an abstract is not a substitute for reading the methods.

Sometimes the correct answer will be narrower than the one we hoped for. That may be disappointing, but it gives us a better place to start the next experiment.

Why this matters for Stemma

We are building toward an assistant that can examine the support beneath a scientific statement, rather than simply attach a plausible-looking reference. That ability is a research goal, not yet a demonstrated general capability.

Reliable scientific work should be able to show its sources, state what follows from them, and leave the unsupported leap visible.
